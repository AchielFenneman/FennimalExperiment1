# Supplementary information: experiment procedure

This supplement describes how the mentalizing experiment was constructed, including the assignment of stimuli and the between-subjects manipulation. The experiment was an online implementation of the Fennimals paradigm. Participants travelled to a fictive island, Fenneland, and looked after three fantasy animals, dubbed Fennimals, together with a partner. The partner was present for the events that established what had been stored in a shared box, and was absent when the contents of that box were later changed. A final phase then measured what participants attributed to the partner, and what they themselves remembered of the earlier events. Recruitment, exclusion, and the statistical analysis are reported separately.

## Materials

### Fenneland and the Fennimals

Fenneland consisted of four visually and thematically distinct regions: the jungle, the village, the north, and the desert. Each region had its own locations, color scheme, and preferred body type. For each participant the software selected three of these four regions.

Each participant met three Fennimals, labelled A, B, and C. These labels name fixed roles. The particular head, name, region, location, toy, box, and sack were assigned anew for each participant, as described under Between-subjects randomization. Fennimal A had a toy, a sack, and a box. Fennimal B had a different toy and was tied to the same box, and had no sack. Fennimal C had a third toy, and had neither a box nor a sack. Because each Fennimal was placed in a different region, the three also differed in body type and color scheme. A Fennimal’s name was the name designated for its head.

The box shared by Fennimals A and B is the box on which the manipulation and the final questions turned. The partner saw Fennimal A’s toy go into Fennimal A’s sack, and saw that sack go into the shared box. Fennimal B’s toy entered the box only later, and only while the partner was away.

### Interactions with the Fennimals

Each visit to a Fennimal took one of the forms below. Which form occurred in which part of the experiment is described in the procedure.

**Playing.** The Fennimal appeared and the software announced the Fennimal’s name. The Fennimal’s toy was then produced, and the participant and the Fennimal played with it. The Fennimal afterwards wandered off and left the toy behind. On the phone-room visits, two questions were asked before the Fennimal was visible: “Which Fennimal lives here?”, with the three faces as options, and “Which toy does this [name] like to play with?”, with the three toys as options. A wrong answer was rejected and the question was asked again.

**Repairing a toy.** The opening was the same as a play visit, including the two questions on the phone-room visits. The toy was then revealed to be broken. The participant comforted the Fennimal and dragged the pieces back into place, with the partner helping when the partner was present. Once the toy was repaired, the Fennimal played with it and wandered off, again leaving the toy behind.

**Storing a toy in a sack.** The toy and the sack were on screen. The participant dragged the toy into the sack. The partner, when present, took part in opening and closing the sack.

**Storing a sack in a box.** The filled sack and the box were on screen. The partner opened the box, and the participant placed the sack inside. When the partner was present for this visit, the software recorded, as the partner’s belief, that the box contained the toy that had been placed in the sack.

**Retrieving a lost box.** The Fennimal who had found the box was met with the box, which was dirty. The participant and the partner cleaned the box together with that Fennimal, and then decorated it together. The software recorded the finder as the Fennimal who had decorated the box. The participant then attached a lost-and-found tag, and was told that somebody would come to collect the box. This visit did not change the partner’s belief about what the box contained.

**Replacing the contents of a box.** The partner arrived with the participant, and the partner’s phone then rang. The partner said: “There’s a minor situation over at [another location]. I’ll go check it out. I’ll leave my backpack here — can you take care of things here while I’m gone?” The participant answered “No problem!”, and the partner left. The backpack contained the box. The participant opened it, took out the box, removed what was already inside, and placed the toy from that visit into the box. The software updated the contents of the box and left the partner’s belief unchanged.

### The partner

The partner was introduced as a guide who would observe the participant’s interactions with the Fennimals and assist where needed. Participants chose their own avatar — its gender, skin color, hair color, and outfit — and could randomize that choice. Whenever an avatar attribute was set, the software gave the partner a different value of the same attribute, drawn from the options the participant had not selected. With two genders available, the partner was therefore always the other gender. Skin color, hair color, and outfit were each drawn from the remaining alternatives, so the partner also differed from the participant on those attributes.

The partner’s name was drawn from a list matched to the partner’s gender, and was redrawn whenever that gender was set. A male partner was named Liam, Matt, Oliver, James, or John. A female partner was named Mary, Jenn, Linda, Susan, or Sarah.

Participants could earn bonus stars during the final phase. Each star was worth $0.10. A correct answer earned one star, including on the two practice questions and on the short matching questions that preceded each partner question. Memory questions earned a star in the same way. The maximum was 16 stars.

## Procedure

The experiment proceeded in a fixed sequence of phases: instructions, a free exploration of the island, a phone-room learning phase, a search for the lost box, a second phone-room phase in which the box was changed while the partner was away, the partner-belief phase, and a short questionnaire.

### Instructions

Participants first confirmed that their browser could run the experiment and were asked to switch to full screen. They then provided informed consent and were asked to complete the experiment in a single sitting, without turning to other windows or screens. They next created their avatar, read an overview of how to travel the island and search its locations, and were introduced to the partner.

### Free exploration

This phase was designed to introduce the three Fennimals and the toys they liked to play with, with the partner present throughout. Before the Fennimals could be searched for, the software required a visit to a watchtower. Participants could then travel freely. Entering a region centered that region on the screen. Coming close to a location that held a Fennimal revealed a search control; holding it down searched the location and allowed the participant to enter. Each visit was a play interaction, as described above. The phase ended when all three Fennimals had been visited. The order of those visits was up to the participant. A finder displayed the Fennimals that had not yet been visited.

### Phone room

Phone-room phases delivered a sequence of visits as calls from a room at the center of the island. Each call named the destination. Closing the call carried the participant there. A completed visit returned them to the phone, unless the phase was set to leave them on the island after its final call.

The first phone-room phase was designed to bind each Fennimal to a toy, and then, with the partner watching, to store Fennimal A’s toy in the shared box. The partner was present throughout. Calls were grouped into four subblocks, run in a fixed order, and shuffled within each subblock. The first subblock was a repair interaction with each of the three Fennimals. The second was a play interaction with each of the three. The third was a store-in-sack interaction with Fennimal A’s toy. The fourth was a store-in-box interaction, placing that sack in the shared box. After this last call, participants remained on the island. The software then held, as the partner’s belief, that the shared box contained Fennimal A’s toy.

### The lost box

The next phase was the between-subjects manipulation. The conditions varied whether the Fennimal who found the shared box was the one whose toy the partner believed to be inside (boost), the one whose toy participants would later place in the box while the partner was away (cost), or a Fennimal tied to neither (neutral). The shared box had gone missing, and participants searched the island in the same manner as during free exploration, again with the partner present and with the finder available, until they came across the assigned Fennimal. That visit was a lost-box retrieval, as described above.

| Condition | Who had found the shared box | Relative weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| boost | Fennimal A | 1 |
| neutral | Fennimal C | 1 |
| cost | Fennimal B | 2 |

The finder was encountered at a location in their own region, other than the location where that Fennimal had been met before.

### Changing the box

A second phone-room phase was designed to change the true contents of the box whilst leaving the partner’s belief in place. It consisted of a single call, concerning a toy of Fennimal B’s that had been left behind, and took participants to a location in Fennimal B’s region. The visit was a replacement interaction, as described above: the partner left for a different location in that region, and participants placed Fennimal B’s toy into the shared box. The partner still believed the box to contain Fennimal A’s toy. Participants then walked back across the island. The partner rejoined them on the way and returned with them to the phone room, and was given no account of what had been done with the box.

### Partner-belief phase

The final phase measured what participants attributed to the partner, and then what they remembered. It was conducted indoors, one question at a time, and a correct answer earned one star. Response time was recorded from the moment the options could be selected.

Two practice questions came first. Each showed a target shape in a target color and three alternatives. The first asked participants to match the shape, the second to match the color. Exactly one alternative followed the requested rule; the other two paired the remaining shapes and colors so that a match on the unrequested dimension was available as a foil. The same kind of question was inserted, once, immediately before each of the four partner questions below. Those four fillers alternated, beginning with shape and then color.

The four partner questions then followed, in a fixed order.

First, the partner stood beside the closed box and participants were asked “Does [partner] know which toy is currently in the [box]?”, answering Yes or No. The correct answer was No: the partner’s belief and the current contents differed.

Second, the partner walked in and faced the curtained box. Participants clicked the curtain, and were asked “What does [partner] believe is in the [box]? Answer as accurately as you can.” The options were the three toys from the experiment together with an empty box, arranged in a circle in a random order. The correct option was Fennimal A’s toy, which was the content the partner had last seen stored.

Third, the partner announced “I’ll go and find the [Fennimal A’s toy]”, turned toward the table, and participants were asked “Where will [partner] first look for the [toy]?” The options were the shared box and the partner’s backpack, in random left–right order. The correct option was the box, because that is where the partner believed the toy to be.

Fourth, the partner was absent. The box was shown with a question mark, and participants were asked “What is really in the [box] right now? Answer as accurately as you can.” The options were again the three toys and an empty box, newly shuffled. The correct option was Fennimal B’s toy.

A short instruction then introduced four memory questions. These were answered without the partner, with about one second between questions, and with no filler questions in between. The questions ran in a fixed order.

1. The closed box was revealed, and participants were asked “Which Fennimal helped decorate this box?” The options were the three Fennimal heads, in random order. The correct head was the Fennimal who had found the box.
2. Each Fennimal was shown in turn, in the order A, then B, then C, and participants were asked “Which toy belongs to this Fennimal?” Each question offered the three toys, in random order.
3. The box was shown, and participants were asked “Which sack did you previously place in this box?” The correct sack was shown together with two sacks that had not been used anywhere in the experiment, drawn from the remaining sacks in the library. The three options were placed in random order.
4. The sack was shown, and participants were asked “Which toy did you previously place in this sack?” The options were the three toys, in random order, and the correct option was Fennimal A’s toy.

### Questionnaire

Participants then reported their age, the gender they identified as, and whether they had any form of color-blindness. A final screen showed the stars earned during the experiment and the corresponding bonus.

## Between-subjects randomization

All random draws were made in the participant’s browser at the start of the session, or at the start of the phase that needed them, and were stored with the session. Refreshing the page reproduced the same assignment. The draws were made in the order below.

**Heads.** Eight head types were eligible: an astronaut, a cupcake, a test tube, a television, a jack-o’-lantern, an elephant, a block, and a parrot. These eight were shuffled, and the first three were assigned to Fennimals A, B, and C. The remaining five were unused. Each assigned head brought its designated name with it; where a head had more than one name on file, the first listed name was used.

**Regions and locations.** The jungle, the village, the north, and the desert were shuffled. The first three of that shuffle were assigned to Fennimals A, B, and C, and the fourth was unused. Within a selected region, the locations were shuffled and the Fennimal was placed at the first of them. Body type and color scheme, for both body and head, were the ones belonging to the assigned region.

**Toys, the box, and the sack.** The toys in the stimulus library were shuffled, and the first three were assigned to Fennimals A, B, and C. The boxes in the library were shuffled, and the first was used as the single shared box. The sacks in the library were shuffled, and the first was used as Fennimal A’s sack.

**Avatars.** As described under The partner, the participant set or randomized their own avatar, and the partner’s gender, skin color, hair color, and outfit were taken from the options the participant had not selected. The partner’s name was then drawn from the five names listed for that gender.

**Order of encounters.** During free exploration, participants chose the order in which they visited the three Fennimals after the watchtower. During the first phone-room phase, the four subblocks ran in the fixed order given above, and the visits inside the repair subblock and inside the play subblock were shuffled. The two storing visits concerned only Fennimal A, so they had no order to shuffle.

**The lost-box condition.** One of boost, neutral, and cost was drawn with relative weights 1, 1, and 2, so that the probabilities were 1/4, 1/4, and 1/2. The condition was drawn once and kept for the rest of the experiment. It determined which Fennimal was encountered with the shared box, and therefore which Fennimal was later the correct answer to the decoration question. It left the partner’s belief, the later replacement, and the toys offered on the belief and reality questions unchanged.

**The final phase.** The two practice questions were fixed as shape, then color. The four filler questions that preceded the partner questions alternated shape and color, beginning with shape. Within each partner question and each memory question, the spatial order of the options was shuffled. The three Fennimal–toy memory questions were asked in the fixed order A, B, C. The two unused sacks on the sack question were a random pair from the sacks that had not been assigned to the experiment.
